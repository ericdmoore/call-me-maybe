# When Call Me Maybe is for you — short narrative drafts

These are fictional illustrative scenes, not customer quotations or evidence
of developmental outcomes. Jonathan Haidt is part of the requested parental
context; his mention implies neither endorsement nor a claim that this product
has been clinically evaluated.

## 1. She wants to call Sophie

You have read Jonathan Haidt. Your nine-year-old daughter has a more immediate
concern: Sophie has a phone, and she would like one too.

At dinner, you ask what she wants to do with it.

“Call Sophie.”

You want that for her. The long, wandering conversations. Making Saturday
plans herself. Hearing a friend laugh without asking to borrow your phone.
You are less ready for everything else a smartphone brings along.

So you put a simple handset in the kitchen. Someone in the house handles the
setup. Together, you write down a few numbers and practice making a call.
Later, she is telling Sophie a story you have already heard twice, except
this version is apparently much funnier.

You keep making dinner.

Call Me Maybe may be for you when the answer you want to give is yes to the
conversation, and someone in your household is willing to look after the
phone system that makes it possible.

*Editorial note: do not imply approved-only outgoing calls. The default
system does not provide that restriction.*

## 2. Grandma does not need another app

Grandma calls on Sunday. She has a phone number she knows, a comfortable
chair, and a very detailed question about the school play.

The kitchen phone rings. Your son answers without finding your mobile,
unlocking it, or calling you into the room. He takes the conversation to the
handset by the sofa. You hear him explain that his role has three lines,
although one is technically a sound effect.

The technology has done its job when nobody needs to discuss it.

Call Me Maybe may be for you when you want familiar calling across different
generations, with the routing handled by your house. Grandma can use an
ordinary phone number; she does not need your software.

## 3. The phone has a bedtime too

The after-school call starts with homework and moves on to an elaborate plan
for a backyard obstacle course. This is exactly why you wanted a home phone.

Before the first evening call, you agree on its bedtime. Your child knows
when to say goodbye. The handset follows the schedule you configured, and
ordinary calls stop for the night.

Tomorrow, the obstacle course can acquire another improbable feature.

Call Me Maybe may be for you when you want a phone to fit the household's
rhythm. Its handset curfew covers ordinary incoming and outgoing calls;
emergency dialing and scheduled reminders remain exceptions. The running
system checks active-call cutoff once a minute, so write “bedtime,” not
“disconnects at the exact second.”

## 4. You already have the person who fixes the Wi-Fi

There is a Raspberry Pi in a drawer and someone in the house who knows why
it is there.

That person wants a small weekend project. Everyone else wants a phone that
rings in the right room. The interesting moment is when those wishes meet:
a friend reaches the kids first, an unanswered call moves to an adult, and
Grandma's message is waiting when everyone gets home.

Nobody else needs to care how Asterisk works. Someone does need to maintain
it, and to leave instructions the next adult can follow.

Call Me Maybe may be for you when you want to choose your own phones and
provider, and have a willing operator at home. If nobody wants that job,
a managed family-phone service is a sensible choice.
